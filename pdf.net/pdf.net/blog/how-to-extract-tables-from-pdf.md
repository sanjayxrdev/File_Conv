## What Type of PDF Do You Have?

There are two significantly different types of PDFs that can affect the table extraction process: **[scanned](https://pdf.net/blog/how-to-edit-scanned-pdfs) and text-based PDFs**. The former is essentially a scanned image in a PDF format, while the latter contains actual, selectable text that can be edited and extracted.

Let’s briefly cover the differences between the two to help you identify them:

| Scanned PDF | Text-based PDF |
| --- | --- |
| Created by scanning a paper or taking a photo of a document | Created from digital apps, like Word or Google Docs |
| Can’t be searched and edited without applying optical character recognition (OCR) | Searchable and editable |
| Large in size | Lightweight and compact |
| Tables and other information cannot be extracted before OCR is applied | Tables and other information can be extracted using regular tools |

So, before you even try to extract a table, **check whether your PDF is scanned or text-based**. If it’s scanned, none of the methods described below will help until you convert the image into machine-encoded text.

Checking is pretty simple, too: try to **select a single word in a PDF**. If you can’t, and an entire line gets selected instead, your document is scanned.

Alternatively, you can **zoom in to over 400%** and inspect the letters; if they remain crisp, it’s a text-based PDF, while blurriness indicates your document is scanned.

Lastly, if your **PDF is password-protected**, you will need to [remove the password](https://pdf.net/unlock-pdf) before attempting any extraction method.

## How to Extract Tables From a Scanned PDF

To extract tables from a scanned PDF, you should first **apply optical character recognition (OCR)** to convert image text into machine-encoded text. If you don’t do this, your file will remain unreadable to most programs, which process it as pixel data, not text.

OCR processes image data and **identifies lines, intersections, and shading**, which helps it isolate individual letters and numbers. Then, it matches them with font libraries and converts them to editable formats.

While OCR is widely regarded as the **most effective way to digitize scanned images**, it has its limitations. Namely, its accuracy depends on the scan quality, which means that high-resolution images produce better results. So, make sure your scan is saved with a [high DPI](https://www.lenovo.com/us/en/glossary/whatis-is-dpi/) (at least 300 DPI) and double-check the result for potential mistakes.

The most important element for successful table extraction may be using PDF files with **high resolution and quality**. Non-standard formats and low-pixelated images can cause issues for converters. Last but not least, you should ensure that everything in your extracted table is correct. Sometimes, there may be parts of your PDF document that your converter could not grasp or that you forgot to preset before conversion. Correcting such minor details will make your tables **picture-perfect**.
