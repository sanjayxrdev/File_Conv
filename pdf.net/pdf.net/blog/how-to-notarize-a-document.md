## What Is Notarization of Documents?

In simple terms, notarization is the **process of making a document official**. Its point is to confirm that the person signing the document truly is who they claim to be, thus preventing fraud. In addition to verifying the signer’s identity, notarization ensures they understand what they’re signing and that they are doing so willingly—not under threat or pressure.

This process makes the document legally recognized and is typically overseen by a notary public—an official authorized by the government to **witness the signing of documents**. They’re impartial and don’t give legal advice—their role is to make sure the signer’s mental capacity is sound so that they can make informed decisions about the document.

At the end of the notarization process, the notary public **adds their seal or stamp to the document** to confirm everything has been done correctly and in accordance with the law.

## 3 Main Types of Notarization

The three main types of notarization include:

1. **Acknowledgements**. These** **are the most common type of notarial act, which involves the notary confirming that the signer is who they claim to be and that they have signed the document willingly. In this case, the document can be signed beforehand, as long as the signer confirms that the signature is theirs.
2. **Jurats**. Also known as sworn statements, jurats confirm that the contents of a certain document are true. In this case, the document must be signed in the notary’s presence.
3. **Copy certifications**. With these, the notary verifies that a copy of an original document is an accurate reproduction. This is the process of document authentication by comparing it to the original.

Now that the definitions are clear, let’s take a look at the differences between these three types of document notarization:

| Acknowledgments | Jurats | Copy certifications |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can be signed before the appointment with the notary | Must be signed in front of the notary | A copy of the signed document |
| Used for real estate, trusts, and loans | Used for depositions and affidavits | Used for copies of various documents |
| The purpose is the authentication of identity and intent | The purpose is the truthfulness of documents and statements | The purpose is the integrity and accuracy of the reproduction |

## How to Notarize a Document in Person: Step-by-Step Guide

![How to Notarize a Document: Step-by-Step Guide](https://cdn.sanity.io/images/i16te7yp/production/7ae2e820d6905a783b68d309a33fab92c4c27c6b-928x616.jpg?fit=max&auto=format)

Let’s examine the necessary steps to notarize a document in person, starting with its preparation and ending with a notary stamp or seal making it official:

### #1. Prepare Your Document

To prepare the document for notarization, read it carefully and **check for any blanks or errors** that could cause problems down the line. Additionally, if the signing of the document calls for witnesses, make sure you have as many as you need and that they’re **available at the time of signing**.

Don’t sign the document at this stage—a notary needs to be present when you do so. Finally, **bring a valid ID** with you since you’ll need it at the time of signing to prove your identity.

### #2. Find a Licensed Notary

Now that your document is ready, it’s time to locate a licensed notary public in your area. They can be found at **banks**, **libraries**, **shipping centers**, or **through notary directories online**. Before you reach out to them and arrange an appointment, make sure they’re authorized to handle your specific type of document in your state.

### #3. Sign the Document in Person

As previously mentioned, **wait until you meet the notary to sign the document**. This way, they can witness your [signature](https://pdf.net/blog/what-is-a-wet-signature) and confirm you’re doing it willingly. If the signing of your document requires witnesses, they also need to be present and ready to sign at this stage. As for the exact procedure, leave it to the notary—they’ll ensure everything is done properly.

### #4. Have the Document Notarized

At this point, the notary will **review your document**, check your ID to **verify your identity**, and **confirm you understand what you’re signing**.

Once everything checks out, they’ll add their own signature and official seal or stamp to the document. This will certify the document as valid and legally recognized, preventing any disputes or fraud moving forward.

### #5. Pay the Notary Fee

When your document receives the official seal or stamp, it’s time to pay the notary fee. The **amount varies depending on the state and type of notarization**, and in some cases, you may be charged per signature or stamp.

To avoid inconveniences, make sure you ask about the fee beforehand and bring the required payment method.

### #6. Receive the Notarized Document

Once your document is officially notarized and the fee is paid, the notary will hand it back to you. At this point, you can use it for whatever purpose you’ve had it notarized and **submit it to a legal office, financial institution, or another party**.

However, before you do that, double-check the notarized document for accuracy and make sure all the signatures and seals are in place.

## How to Notarize a Document Online

You can notarize a document online through [**remote online notarization (RON)**](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/arizona/title-2/chapter-12/article-13). It makes the process simpler, easier, and faster, as it allows you to complete it from the comfort of your home.

Unlike in-person notarization, RON connects you with a licensed official through a **video call**. This method is just as valid and secure as traditional notarization and is legal in many states. Needless to say, it’s especially convenient for people with busy schedules and in situations when meeting with a notary in person isn’t practical.

Let’s see how to notarize a [PDF file](https://pdf.net/blog/what-does-pdf-format-mean) online step by step:

1. **Find an online notary service**. Choose a reputable platform offering notary services and make sure it’s legally recognized in your state.
2. **Upload your document**. Scan or upload the fully prepared but unsigned document to the platform.
3. **Verify your identity**. Upload your ID and answer security questions to complete an identity check.
4. **Join a video call**. Meet with the notary virtually and have them review your document and watch as you [sign it electronically](https://pdf.net/blog/how-to-make-an-electronic-signature).
5. **Receive the notarized document**. After the notary adds their electronic signature and seal to the document, you can download it.

## 3 Mistakes to Avoid During the Notarization Process

The most common mistakes to keep an eye out for during the notarization process include signing the document too early, using invalid identification, and failing to notice the document is incorrect or incomplete.

Let’s explore them more closely:

### #1. Signing the Document Too Early

As previously mentioned, signing the document before meeting with the notary defies the whole purpose of notarization. Namely, the **notary needs to witness you signing the document** to ensure you’re doing it willingly and knowingly and verify the signature’s authenticity.

With this in mind, if you sign the document too early, it’ll likely get rejected, forcing you to go through the process again. To avoid this, wait until you meet the notary, whether online or in person, to have them watch you sign the document.

### #2. Using Invalid Identification

Next to the paperwork you need notarized, your ID is the most important document you must have with you when meeting with a notary. An expired or incorrect ID, like a school card instead of a government-issued one, will either cause a delay in the process or a rejection of your document.

Therefore, make sure your **ID is up to date**,** includes a clear photo**, and **matches the name on the document**. As for the type, you can bring a passport, driver’s license, or ID card.

### #3. Incorrect or Incomplete Documents

[Missing pages](https://pdf.net/blog/pdf-missing-text), blank spaces, errors, and typos can make your document invalid. Namely, notaries can’t notarize incomplete and incorrect documents. To save yourself the hassle of repeating the process, review the document carefully before meeting with the notary. See if all **required information is filled out** and check for any **missing sections**.
