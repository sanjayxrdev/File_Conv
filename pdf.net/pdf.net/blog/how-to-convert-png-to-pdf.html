How to Convert PNG to PDF Documents: A Guide for Employees

Learn how to convert PNG to PDF documents for work; our guide covers the necessary tools, page ordering, compression, and troubleshooting.

You can convert PNG to PDF online, with pdf.net, or with one of the built-in tools on Windows or Mac, and the choice depends on the OS and device you’re using, as well as on your needs.

If there’s a folder of PNG screenshots or images that need to go to a colleague, a client, or a submission portal as a single document, emailing all of them separately in this situation is not practical. How to Convert PNG to PDF With Built-In Tools

To convert PNG to PDF with built-in tools, you can choose between two solid native options, depending on whether you use Mac or Windows.

#1. Windows: Microsoft Print to PDF

This method works by printing each PNG file to a virtual PDF printer. Open the image in the Photos app, press Ctrl+P, select "Microsoft Print to PDF" as the printer, and save. Repeat for each image. Once you have individual PDFs for every PNG, use a merge PDF tool to combine them into one file.

This approach is practical for two or three images; yet, for ten or more, it becomes repetitive. The Print to PDF method also does not give you control over page margins or output resolution at the point of conversion.

#2. macOS: Preview

Here are the steps you should follow to convert your PNGs in Preview:

  1. Select all your PNG files in Finder (Shift+Click or Cmd+Click), right-click, and choose Open With > Preview.
  2. All images open as a single document with a thumbnail sidebar. Drag the thumbnails to reorder pages.
  3. Go to File and choose Export as PDF.

This method preserves the order you set in the sidebar and is reliable for small to medium batches. However, large batches may slow Preview down on older hardware.

Before sending, verify the page order and check the file size. Preview's exported PDFs can be large if the source images are high resolution. If the file exceeds your sending limit, compress it afterward.

What Should You Check Before You Convert PNGs to PDF?

Before converting PNGs to PDF, you should check file order, image orientation, resolution, and transparent backgrounds. Catching these issues beforehand takes a few minutes and saves you from re-uploading and re-ordering pages after the fact, especially with larger batches.

#1. File Order

Most operating systems list files alphabetically or by date modified. If your PNGs are named things like “screenshot1.png”, “screenshot2.png”, the order is predictable; if they are named by timestamp or with inconsistent naming, rename them sequentially before uploading. Doing this before conversion is faster than reordering pages afterward.

#2. Orientation

A landscape PNG placed into a portrait PDF page will either be rotated automatically (which may not match the original intent) or left as-is with large white margins. Review your images before converting and identify any that were captured horizontally. You can rotate the document and its pages after conversion, but confirming orientation beforehand saves time.

#3. Resolution and File Size

Higher resolution PNGs produce sharper PDFs but significantly larger files, so a 10-image PDF where each PNG was a 4K screenshot can easily reach 30-50 MB.

Most email systems cap attachments at 10-25 MB, so if your output is likely to exceed sending limits, plan to compress the PDF after conversion rather than reducing image quality before. This gives you more control over where quality is sacrificed.

#4. Transparent Backgrounds

PNG supports transparency, but PDF does not handle it the same way. When a transparent PNG is embedded in a PDF, the transparent areas are typically rendered as white.

If your images have logos, icons, or design elements with transparent backgrounds, they will appear against a white page in the final PDF. This is usually acceptable for work documents, but worth knowing in advance if your PNGs were designed for dark backgrounds.

When the PDF File is Too Large to Send

A PDF file is usually too large to send if its size exceeds the recipient’s portal limitation (e.g., the resulting PDF is 40 MB, and the recipient's portal only accepts 10 MB). This is expected behavior when source images are high-resolution screenshots or photos.

The right approach is to compress the PDF after conversion rather than degrade the source images using pdf.net’s tool, which offers multiple compression levels.

Choose a balanced setting first and check whether the output is still legible. For documents that contain only images (no embedded text), moderate compression usually reduces file size by 40-70% without visible quality loss on screen.

One genuine trade-off: aggressive compression can blur fine lines and small text within screenshots. If your PNGs contain annotated code, legal text, or detailed forms, use the lightest compression setting that still meets the file size requirement for email or any other channel.

In case even light compression makes the content unreadable, consider splitting the batch into two PDFs and sending them separately rather than compressing to the point of illegibility.

5 Common Problems When Converting PNGs for Work

Batch conversion is usually straightforward, but a handful of issues show up more often than others, including wrong page order, washed-out colors, rotated pages, oversized files, and blurry images. Most of these come from the source PNGs rather than the conversion process itself, and each one has a quick fix.

  • Pages are in the wrong order. This is the most common complaint after batch conversion. The fix depends on where it happened. If you are using an online tool, most include a drag-to-reorder interface before you generate the final PDF. If you already have the combined PDF, use a page rearrangement tool to correct the sequence. To prevent the problem entirely, rename your PNG files with a numeric prefix (01_, 02_, 03_) before uploading.
  • Colors look washed out, or the background is wrong. Transparent PNGs default to a white background in PDF. If your PNG had a colored or dark background via transparency, it will appear white in the PDF. If the PNG itself has a colored background (not transparent), the color should transfer correctly. Check whether the issue is transparency or a profile mismatch. Most online converters embed images in sRGB, which matches standard display environments.
  • Pages are rotated 90 degrees. This typically happens when a screenshot taken on a mobile device retains landscape EXIF rotation metadata that the converter does not interpret. The fix is to rotate the affected pages after conversion.
  • The file is too large to email or upload. Compress your PDF as described above, but also check whether any of your source PNGs were unnecessarily large before conversion. Scaling down images to the resolution actually needed (e.g., 1200px wide for a document screenshot) before converting can reduce output file size significantly without visible quality loss in the PDF.
  • Some pages look blurry. The PDF cannot add resolution that was not in the original PNG. If your source screenshots were captured at a small size and then upscaled, the blurriness is permanent. The solution is to re-export or re-capture the images at a higher resolution. For screen captures, capturing at 1x rather than with display scaling can sometimes produce cleaner results depending on the application.
