## ESIGN Act: Background and Purpose

![ESIGN Act: Background and Purpose](https://cdn.sanity.io/images/i16te7yp/production/db7cab3e3908f1da3418df04cb1b79c20d4f4dcc-545x360.jpg?fit=max&auto=format)

The ESIGN Act was introduced after the UETA in 1999, with the purpose to resolve legal uncertainty amidst the growth of digital transactions. It went into effect in 2000 and has worked in tandem with the UETA ever since.

### The Legislative History Behind Electronic Signature Laws

By the late 1990s, the growth of the Internet and e-commerce revealed major limitations in existing signature laws. Businesses frequently encountered legal uncertainty because state laws varied drastically, and this dampened economic prosperity.

In 1999, the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA) was introduced as a model for recognizing electronic signatures and records at the state level. However, because digital commerce continued to grow across state boundaries, Congress acted to establish a national standard.

On June 30, 2000, [President Bill Clinton signed the ESIGN Act](https://clintonwhitehouse4.archives.gov/WH/New/html/electronic_signatures.html), which went into effect on October 1, 2000. **The purpose of the ESIGN Act was to validate electronic signatures for transactions that occur in or affect interstate or foreign commerce.**

### Core Principles and Legal Framework

According to the ESIGN Act:

- _“A signature, contract, or other record relating to such transaction may not be denied legal effect, validity, or enforceability solely because it is in electronic form.” (Section 101 (a)(1))_
- _“A contract relating to such transaction may not be denied legal effect, validity, or enforceability solely because an electronic signature or electronic record was used in its formation.” (Section 101 (a)(2))_

This means the ESIGN Act ensures that an electronic signature, or e-signature, carries the same legal weight as a handwritten one. However, certain requirements must be met. These include** intent to sign, consent to conduct business electronically, and reliable record retention.**

The ESIGN Act defines an “electronic signature” as an electronic sound, symbol, or process attached to or logically associated with a contract or other record that has been adopted or executed by a person with the intent to sign and record. (Section 106 (5))

Furthermore, a digital signature using public key encryption technology or an appellation of a person’s name at the end of an email would still qualify as an electronic signature. This is true as long as the signer adopted or executed the “symbol,” and it identified the signer.

### Federal vs. State Jurisdiction and the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act

**While ESIGN is a federal law, it works with the UETA**, which has been adopted by 49 states. According to the UETA,

- _“If a law requires a record to be in writing, an electronic record satisfies the law.” (Section 107-12A)_
- _“If a law requires a signature, an electronic signature satisfies the law.”_ _(Section 107-12A)_

New York is the exception, but it maintains its own set of e-signature rules under [the Electronic Signatures and Records Act (ESRA)](https://its.ny.gov/electronic-signatures-and-records-act-esra-regulation).

The UETA is different from the ESIGN Act in that it is a model state law. This means that every state in the U.S. can adopt and tailor it to their specific legal requirements. However, each state is still required to have some type of law that regulates the [legal validity of electronic signatures](https://pdf.net/blog/is-electronic-signature-legal).

Together, the UETA and the ESIGN Act establish a uniform framework for electronic transactions in the United States. This way, they give businesses and consumers legal certainty when using e-signatures.

## How to Comply with the ESIGN Act

To comply with the ESIGN Act, you must meet several conditions and understand how ESIGN works in real-world digital transactions.

First, **the ESIGN Act requires affirmative consent.** Before any [PDF document is signed](https://pdf.net/blog/how-to-sign-pdfs-digitally), the person signing has to clearly agree to conduct business electronically. They must also be informed of disclosures outlining their rights, which include the rights to:

- Request a paper version of the electronically signed document
- Understand the fees associated with requesting paper versions of records
- Withdraw consent at any time
- Understand the hardware or software needed to access electronic records

Additionally, **there must be reliable recordkeeping.** Any electronic record, defined as a contract or other record created, generated, sent, communicated, received, or stored electronically (Section 106 (4)), must accurately reflect the original document and remain accessible to all authorized parties.

**The document must also be reproducible in a legible format**, such as a signed PDF, and retained for the legally required period.

The ESIGN Act allows for the use of “electronic agents,” such as a computer program, an electronic, or another automated signature tool, as long as the signature can be attributed to a person.

If you are wondering whether there are some documents where electronic signatures are not accepted, the answer is yes.** Some documents are explicitly excluded, which means they can’t be signed electronically under ESIGN** and still require [wet signatures](https://pdf.net/blog/what-is-a-wet-signature). These include:

- Wills
- Testamentary trusts
- Codicils
- Court orders, notices, or official court documents like briefs and pleadings required for court proceedings
- Legal notices involving adoption, divorce, or other family law matters
- Notices of utility service termination, such as those for water, heat, and power
- Recalls of products, notices of products’ material failures, and documents required for handling hazardous materials like pesticides
- Cancellations of health or life insurance benefits (Excluding annuities)

In the context of insurance, the purpose of the ESIGN Act is to streamline digital interactions between providers and policyholders. It allows insurance companies to obtain consent, deliver policies, and collect signatures electronically. However, it still needs to meet the requirements outlined above.

An insurance agent or broker that enters into an electronically signed contract may not be held liable for any deficiency in the electronic procedures agreed upon by the parties. This applies as long as the agent or broker has not been negligent, reckless, or tortious and did not deviate from the procedures.

If a [document has to be notarized](https://www.notarize.com/blog/what-does-it-mean-to-have-a-document-notarized-with-a-notary-public), acknowledged, verified, or signed under oath, it can be done electronically. The notary can e-sign it, and it will be legally binding as long as all the other necessary information, like commission details or dates, is included.

## What is the ESIGN Act FAQ

### #1. Are electronic signatures created under the ESIGN Act accepted in all 50 states?

Electronic signatures created under the ESIGN Act are **accepted in all 50 states.** 49 states also follow UETA, which works in tandem with the ESIGN Act. New York follows ESRA, which is a separate state law, but similarly validates the legality of e-signatures.

### #2. What types of documents cannot be signed electronically under the ESIGN Act?

The types of documents that cannot be signed electronically under the ESIGN Act include **wills, codicils, testamentary trusts, legal notices involving family law matters, utility shut-off notices, court notices and orders, cancellations of health and life insurance benefits, and recalls of products, notices of products’ material failures, and records involving hazardous material.**

### #3. How does the ESIGN Act protect consumers in electronic transactions?

The ESIGN Act protects consumers in electronic transactions by **mandating consent and disclosures.** Consumers must agree to sign documents electronically and get details about their rights, like how to request paper copies, how to withdraw consent, and how to access hardware or software for their electronic records.

### #4. Can businesses be held liable for improper electronic signature implementation?

**Yes, businesses can be held liable **for improper implementation of electronic signatures. Consequences for violating the ESIGN Act include both compliance risks and reputational risks.
