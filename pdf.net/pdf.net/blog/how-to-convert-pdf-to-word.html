How to Convert PDF to Word Without Losing Formatting

Learn how to convert a PDF to Word while preserving formatting. Simple steps for desktop, Mac, and mobile, plus fixes for common formatting issues.

Alex Chen

Technical Content Specialist

Table of contents

To convert PDF to Word without losing formatting, you need to upload your file to an online converter, like pdf.net, or open it directly in Microsoft Word, which can read and reformat most PDF files on its own. Both methods turn a locked PDF into an editable Word document in under a minute, without any extra software.

PDF files are great for sharing, but they’re not built for editing. If you need to update a resume, revise a contract, or reuse a report, converting it to Word gives you something you can actually work with.

This guide explains exactly why formatting loss happens, how to diagnose your PDF before you convert, and what to do inside Word when the output still isn't right. The steps apply whether you're working on Windows, Mac, or converting entirely in the browser.

Key Takeaways

  • Before converting, you should identify your PDF type (native text, scanned/image-based, or complex layout), since this determines which conversion method would be the most appropriate.
  • Scanned or image-based PDFs require OCR to become editable text; a standard conversion alone won’t work on them.
  • You can export a PDF to Word using Microsoft Word’s built-in function or an online PDF-to-Word tool, like pdf.net, which works on any device.
  • Converting a PDF this way makes it easier to edit text, reuse content, and adjust formatting without retyping the entire document.
  • Some conversion problems you may encounter include formatting shifts, missing fonts, misaligned images, and scanned PDFs that come out as unreadable text.
  • Using a high-quality, text-based source PDF and checking your document after conversion helps you get the cleanest possible Word file.

Identify Your PDF Type Before You Convert

Before opening any tool, identify your PDF type. The conversion approach changes entirely depending on what the file actually contains.

  • Native PDF with selectable text. Open the PDF and try to highlight a word. If you can select characters, the file contains real text data. Conversion preserves paragraph structure well, though fonts and spacing still need a post-conversion check.
  • Scanned or image-based PDF. If clicking on text selects the entire page as an image, there is no underlying text layer. You must use a converter with Optical Character Recognition (OCR) to reconstruct the text and layout into an editable Word document.
  • Complex layout PDF. Multi-column reports, highly designed brochures, or documents with text boxes and floating graphics lose the most fidelity. Even with a high-quality converter, expect to rebuild some structural elements in Word.

No converter is perfect for complex layouts. Setting that expectation upfront saves time, because knowing which element will break lets you focus cleanup efforts.

What Elements Break the Most Often and Why?

Tables, headers and footers, multi-column layouts, rotated text, and embedded fonts break most often during PDF-to-Word conversion, because each relies on fixed positioning that Word's flowing text structure has to reinterpret from scratch.

#1. Tables

Tables are the highest-risk element in any PDF-to-Word conversion. The problems cluster around three specific scenarios:

  1. Merged cells. A table header that spans four columns in the PDF frequently converts into four separate single cells, breaking the visual structure.
  2. Borderless tables. Many PDFs use invisible grid layouts for alignment purposes. After conversion, these appear as irregularly spaced text rather than a table at all.
  3. Multi-page tables. A table that continues across two pages may split into two separate tables in Word, requiring manual merging.

For table-heavy documents, consider using a dedicated approach to extract tables from PDF separately rather than converting the entire document, then rebuilding the table structure in Word with correct formatting applied from scratch.

#2. Images and figures

Charts, logos, and embedded graphics typically survive conversion as embedded images in the DOCX file, but sizing and positioning often shift. Inline images may become floating objects anchored to unexpected positions, causing text to wrap around them incorrectly.

If the image quality in the converted Word document looks degraded, that degradation usually originates in the PDF itself. If the original PDF used compressed JPEG images, the converter cannot reconstruct resolution that was never present.

For documents where image positioning matters, converting a page of PDF to PNG or JPEG first gives you source images at full resolution that can be manually inserted into Word.

#3. Footnotes, endnotes, and page numbers

Footnotes embedded in the PDF's typesetting structure rarely carry over as proper Word footnotes. They typically appear as inline text at the bottom of each page or as a separate text run.

On the other hand, page numbers in headers and footers often detach and appear as static text rather than as Word's dynamic page numbering fields.

#4. Text boxes and columns

Content inside text boxes in a PDF is treated as a separate text container. After conversion, these often become Word text boxes with fixed positions, which can overlap body text or float off the page when the document is edited.

Multi-column sections are similarly fragile. If the visual layout is critical, it is more reliable to recreate these structural elements in Word using proper column formatting or page layout tools rather than trying to salvage the converted version.

How to Convert PDF to Word on Desktop (Windows)

Converting a PDF to Word on a desktop is simple. Here’s how it works:

  1. Open a blank document in Microsoft Word on your computer and click on File.
  2. Go to Open, choose the folder where the PDF is, and select it.
  3. Word will show the contents of the document and a message saying it will convert the PDF into an editable Word document. Select OK.
  4. Once the file opens, review the formatting and make any needed edits.
  5. When you’re done, click on File, choose Save As, and select the .docx format from the drop-down menu.

If you don’t have Word installed for any reason, you can upload the document to pdf.net via your browser and download the converted file directly, without any software required.

How to Convert PDF to Word on Mac

To convert a PDF to Word on a Mac, you should follow a procedure that is practically identical to one for Windows; the only difference is the interface. Here’s the quickest built-in method:

  3. Word will convert the file automatically and open it as an editable document.
  4. Review the formatting, then go to File > Save As and choose Word Document (.docx).

How to Convert Scanned PDFs

For scanned or image-based files, OCR is not optional; it is the conversion. The process involves analyzing each page image, identifying character shapes, reconstructing words and lines, and then building a structured document around that recognized text.

The quality of OCR output depends heavily on scan quality. A 300 DPI or higher scan with strong contrast between text and background produces reliable results. Low-resolution scans (under 150 DPI), skewed pages, or documents with heavy background textures generate recognition errors that need manual correction.

If you’re in doubt about how to scan one or multiple pages into one PDF, follow these simple steps:

  • Prepare your documents. Remove staples or paper clips, and make sure pages are clean, flat, and in the correct order.
  • Choose your scanning method. Use a flatbed/document-feed scanner, a multifunction printer, or a scanning app on your phone.
  • Set the resolution. Select at least 300 DPI to ensure text stays sharp enough for accurate OCR later.
  • Scan each page. Feed pages through the scanner (or capture them one at a time with a mobile app), checking that each image is straight and well-lit.
  • Combine into a single file. Most scanner software and scanning apps offer a "combine" or "multi-page" mode that automatically merges PDF pages into one.
  • Review the output. Open the merged PDF and check page order, orientation, and image clarity before saving.

4 Pro Tips for the Best PDF Output

A few small habits can make the difference between a clean conversion and one that needs heavy cleanup. Therefore, you should keep these four tips in mind:

  1. Use high-quality PDFs. A clean, well-formatted source file converts more accurately than one with messy layouts or low-resolution scans.
  2. Check formatting after download. Always open the converted file and skim it for shifted text, missing sections, or misplaced images before you rely on it. If you spot leftover formatting issues, our PDF editor can help you fix them quickly.
  3. Use editable source PDFs when possible. If you created the original PDF from a Word document, track down that source file instead of converting the PDF backward.
  4. Convert Word back to PDF when you’re done. Once you’ve finished editing, it’s worth converting your file back to PDF for sharing, since PDFs keep their formatting locked in place across devices.

When PDF to Word Conversion Is a Wrong Approach

Some PDF files should not be converted to Word at all.

If the only change needed is correcting a typo, updating a date, or replacing a single word, editing the PDF directly avoids the entire conversion-and-cleanup cycle. Direct PDF editing keeps the original layout completely intact and takes a fraction of the time.

For highly designed brochures, marketing materials, or print-ready documents with complex layering, converting the whole file produces a heavily distorted DOCX that requires more work to fix than to recreate.

In this case, a better approach is to extract the specific content you need (a data table, a section of body text) as selected text, and paste that into an existing Word template.

For documents where only specific pages are relevant, extracting those pages first simplifies the conversion. This reduces noise from irrelevant sections and improves output quality.

Final Thoughts

Converting a PDF to Word doesn’t have to be complicated, regardless of whether you need to do it from a desktop, a Mac, or your phone. Built-in tools like Word’s Save As function work well for simple files, but an online converter, such as pdf.net, handles larger or more complex documents without any extra software.

If your conversion doesn’t come out clean the first time, most issues, like formatting shifts or missing fonts, are quick to fix once you know what caused them. Keep a high-quality source file on hand, and you’ll get a reliable Word document every time.

How to Convert PDF to Word FAQs

#1. Will my formatting stay the same after conversion?

Your formatting will mostly stay the same after conversion if you use a reliable converter and a clean source PDF, though minor shifts in spacing or fonts are common with complex layouts. Always review the converted document and make small adjustments as needed before you consider it final.

#2. Do I need software to convert a PDF to Word?

You don’t need software to convert a PDF to Word, since online tools like pdf.net handle the entire process in your browser. If you already have Microsoft Word installed, you can also open a PDF directly through the File menu, and Word will convert it automatically.

#3. Is PDF-to-Word conversion secure?

PDF to Word conversion is secure as long as you use a trusted tool that encrypts your file during upload. Look for converters that use HTTPS encryption (TLS protocol) and clearly state their data retention policy, so your document isn’t stored longer than necessary.

#4. How do I convert PDF tables to Word without messing up borders or cells?

Table fidelity depends heavily on the table's original structure. Borderless tables and merged-cell headers are the most error-prone. After conversion, use Word's Convert Text to Table function for any text that should be a table, manually restore merged cells, and apply a consistent table style to rebuild borders.

#5. Can I preserve image quality when converting PDF to Word?

Yes, you can in most cases. Converters retain embedded images at the resolution they exist in the PDF. If the source images were compressed when the PDF was created, the converter cannot restore that lost resolution. For high-fidelity images, extract them from the PDF at full resolution separately, then insert them into Word manually.

#6. Why does my converted Word document show boxes instead of editable text?

This indicates that the PDF was image-based (scanned) and the converter did not apply OCR. The boxes are embedded page images. Re-run the conversion using a tool that explicitly supports OCR, or apply OCR to the PDF first and then convert the resulting file.

#7. Does this converter work on Mac the same as on Windows?

Yes, browser-based converters like pdf.net work identically across Mac and Windows since they run without platform-specific software dependencies. The Word cleanup steps also apply to Microsoft Word on macOS, where the same paragraph, table, and font tools are available.
