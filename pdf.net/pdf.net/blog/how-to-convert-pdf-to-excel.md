## 5 Common Problems When Converting PDF to Excel

Common problems when converting PDF to Excel include **broken table structures, missing data, formatting loss, and unrecognized fonts**. Below, we’ll examine these issues in more detail and recommend some effective solutions.

### #1. Broken Table Structure

When you convert a PDF to Excel, you may face issues with **tables not showing up clearly as they do in the original document**. Instead, the columns may be misaligned, the cells merged, and the text all mashed up, making the table appear confusing.

This generally happens because PDF isn’t designed as a data source format, so the tables that appear in it may not be tagged as such. As a result, they only visually look like tables, but aren’t recognized by the software.

When this happens, you can use the **[Power Query](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/power-query/power-query-what-is-power-query) **editor in Excel to fix the table. Access it through the **Navigator** window by clicking on **Transform Data**. This editor allows you to align and rename columns, combine tables, remove unnecessary rows, and more.

If this doesn’t help, you can always manually fix your table. However, depending on its size, this can be a lengthy and tedious process.

### #2. Missing Data

The issue with missing data usually occurs when the PDF you’re trying to convert to Excel is actually a **scanned image rather than selectable text**. When that’s the case, Excel can’t read the data, and the sheet ends up completely blank.

To fix this, use an optical character recognition (OCR) tool to convert the scanned image into text, then import the data. You can do this in Adobe Acrobat or by using online OCR tools; whichever you choose, the process is usually quite simple.

Alternatively, you can take a screenshot of the PDF tables you want to convert, go to the **Data** tab, then **Get Data**, and click **From Picture**. After that, select your desired picture and let Excel do the rest. Instead of a screenshot, you can also use our [PDF to JPG](https://pdf.net/pdf-to-jpg) tool for the same result.

### #3. Formatting Loss

It can be difficult to transfer tables from a PDF to Excel and preserve formatting because **the two file types store information in completely different ways**. Essentially, PDF doesn’t use real tables, but rather text and visuals at specific coordinates. On the other hand, Excel uses actual rows, columns, and cells.

So, it’s hardly surprising that the formatting and the overall table integrity may be compromised during the conversion. In a way, Excel has to guess where to place certain data and how to format it, as it has no clear instructions.

Though there is no universal fix, you can do several things to ensure the formatting stays as close to the original as possible. Look for PDFs with simple and consistent tables that are tagged as such and digitally generated. **Use OCR and PDF editors** to make your file as Excel-friendly as possible before converting.

### #4. Unrecognized Fonts

If your PDF contains **rare and unusual fonts or unembedded characters**, there’s a chance that Excel won’t recognize them. In that case, it might substitute them with garbled text, and your Excel data might become unreadable.

To prevent this from happening, make sure that the PDF you’re trying to convert to Excel contains common fonts and that they are all embedded properly. This is easy to do by using the **Print **function: open your PDF in Google Chrome, press **CTRL+P **(or** CMD+P on Mac**), and choose **Save as PDF**. The new version of your document will be flattened, with all fonts embedded.

## 4 Tips for Better Conversion Results

Here are four tips to help you achieve better PDF to Excel conversion results:

1. **Use built-in tools, like Power Query**. Aside from making it easy to import PDF data into Excel, Power Query also comes with numerous features that allow you to adjust and clean your tables. So, even if the conversion isn’t perfect initially, you can improve it with this tool.
2. **Ensure your PDF isn’t a scanned image**. Excel can’t read data from a [scanned PDF](https://pdf.net/blog/how-to-edit-scanned-pdfs), so it’s likely that none of the tables will be converted at all. You can check by trying to select a few words in your PDF; if you can’t, it’s scanned.
3. **Use [simple fonts](https://pdf.net/blog/how-to-change-font-in-pdf) and formatting**. The more complex and stylized a PDF is, the less likely your result is to be good. So, make the necessary changes in our PDF editor to make the document more Excel-friendly.
4. **Test different tools**. Power Query may be a simple, built-in solution, but it’s not always the right one. Try an online converter, like pdf.net, to see if it gives a better result, and then choose what suits you more.
