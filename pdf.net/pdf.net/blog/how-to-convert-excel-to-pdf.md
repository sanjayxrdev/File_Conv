## Why Convert Excel to PDF?

You should convert Excel to PDF whenever you want to **share**, **print**, or **archive a spreadsheet without letting anyone change its contents**. A PDF locks your layout, numerical values, and formatting in place, so the file looks identical no matter which device or software opens it.

That said, here are the five main reasons to convert Excel to PDF:

1. **Preserve formatting. **Converting an Excel spreadsheet to PDF keeps your fonts, colors, and cell borders exactly as designed, so a recipient's version of Excel never renders things differently.
2. **Prevent accidental edits. **Once you export Excel to PDF, recipients cannot change your numbers, formulas, or layout unless you explicitly allow it.
3. **Simplify sharing. **Instead of sending a workbook with multiple tabs, you deliver one static file that opens the same way on any device, including phones and tablets.
4. **Standardize printing. **A PDF locks your print area and page breaks, so what you see on screen is exactly what prints, with no surprise [blank pages](https://pdf.net/blog/why-is-my-pdf-printing-blank) or clipped columns.
5. **Support recordkeeping. **Banks, HR departments, and government agencies widely accept PDF for invoices, reports, and applications, which makes converting your Excel file to PDF a practical step before submitting documents. If your spreadsheet contains payroll or financial data, consider [password-protecting the PDF](https://pdf.net/blog/how-to-password-protect-a-pdf) first.

## Excel to PDF Conversion vs. Printing an Excel Spreadsheet

Saving Excel as PDF and printing a spreadsheet **both produce a fixed**, **unchangeable copy**, **but** **solve different problems**. Printing gives you a physical page, while converting to PDF gives you a digital file you can store, email, or upload without using any paper.

Here’s a quick breakdown of how these two processes differ:

|  | Convert to PDF | Print a Physical Copy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Format** | Digital file | Paper document |
| **Sharing** | Instant, via email or upload | Requires scanning or mailing |
| **Storage** | Takes up no physical space | Needs filing or archiving space |
| **Editability** | Locked, but source stays editable | Not editable at all |
| **Cost** | Free with most tools | Ink, paper, and printer costs |

If you mainly need a spreadsheet for reference or sharing, converting to PDF is almost always the faster, cheaper option. Printing still makes sense when a [wet signature](https://pdf.net/blog/what-is-a-wet-signature) or paper filing is required.

## How to Keep Excel Formatting When Saving as PDF

Getting Excel to PDF without losing formatting comes down to **four settings**: margins, orientation, print area, and gridlines.

### #1. Margins

Set your margins under **Page Layout > Margins** before converting, so tables and charts don't get pushed onto extra pages. Narrow margins usually work best for wide spreadsheets, while normal margins suit standard reports. Checking this setting first saves you from re-exporting the file later.

![How to Keep Excel Formatting When Saving as PDF](https://cdn.sanity.io/images/i16te7yp/production/5bc01b125ccb5d0cddb6a6914cdd0f3cc93f2aac-891x282.png?fit=max&auto=format)

### #2. Page Orientation

Wide spreadsheets often need landscape orientation to avoid columns spilling onto a second page. Switch this under **Page Layout > Orientation**, and preview the result before exporting so nothing gets cut off. If a page still comes out sideways after conversion, you can [rotate the PDF pages](https://pdf.net/rotate-pdf) afterward instead of starting the export over.

![Spreadsheet landscape orientation to avoid columns spilling onto a second page](https://cdn.sanity.io/images/i16te7yp/production/94c32d60d7a35eab6a92b4afd82e573d243055c0-774x276.png?fit=max&auto=format)

### #3. Print Area

Define your print area under **Page Layout > Print Area > Set Print Area** to control exactly which cells appear in the PDF. Without this step, Excel may export blank rows or unrelated tabs along with your data, which is one of the most common formatting complaints after conversion.

![Set Print Area to control exactly which cells appear in the PDF](https://cdn.sanity.io/images/i16te7yp/production/bd0ba61a148b1e8bf9cd026390a91382fc4b0998-783x289.png?fit=max&auto=format)

### #4. Gridlines

Decide whether to show or hide gridlines under the **Sheet Options** section of the **Page Layout tab**. Gridlines can make dense tables easier to read, but hiding them often looks cleaner for reports meant for clients or executives.

![Show or hide gridlines under the Sheet Options section of the Page Layout tab](https://cdn.sanity.io/images/i16te7yp/production/7c818722dc327f2a0c7afa883afe061f0429ec8a-1240x258.png?fit=max&auto=format)

## How to Convert Excel to PDF FAQs

### #1. Can I convert XLSX to PDF without Microsoft Excel?

**Yes**,** you can convert XLSX to PDF without Microsoft Excel **by using Google Sheets or an online converter like pdf.net. Both accept XLSX files directly and produce a PDF in a few clicks, so you don't need Excel installed on your device.

### #2. Why does my Excel PDF cut off columns?

Your Excel PDF cuts off columns because the **print area or page orientation wasn't set correctly before exporting**. Switch to landscape orientation for wide spreadsheets and confirm your print area covers every column you want to include.

### #3. Will charts and graphs appear in the PDF?

**Yes**, **charts and graphs appear in the PDF as static images** once you convert your spreadsheet. They keep their appearance, but they lose interactivity, so viewers can't hover over data points or edit the underlying values.
